Process X-ray · IT help desk

Tickets take 40 days to close. The fix takes 6.

We ran our Process X-ray on 4,580 real support tickets (21,348 logged steps, 2010–2014) from the IT help desk of an Italian software company. We turned the log into a process graph and a handover network between 22 staff accounts. Here's where the time goes, and what we'd fix first.

What is a Process X-ray?

A two-week analysis of the activity log your business systems already keep (ERP, ticketing, order or claims). Like an X-ray, it shows what happens inside a process without opening anything up: no new software, no access to your systems, just one data export. This page is an example on public data, so you can see exactly what you'd get.

Exhibit 2 · Where the time goes

72% of all ticket time is a waiting window after the fix

After a ticket is resolved, it stays open for a fixed confirmation window before it closes. That window was about 30 days until 2012; the data shows it dropped to 15 days in 2013. It isn't work, but it makes "time to close" look more than six times longer than "time to fix" in every report.

Exhibit 4 · What makes a ticket slow

Three patterns separate fast tickets from stuck ones

Median days to fix, comparing tickets with and without each pattern. These are associations in the data, not proof of cause, but they show where to look first.

Waiting to be picked up

Median pickup is 6 hours, but 29% of tickets wait over 3 days, and those hold 92% of all queue time.

Parked in "Wait" status

27% of tickets get parked. They take 18.9 days to fix against 2.8 for the rest.

Passed between people

Every handover before the fix multiplies the time. Tickets solved by the first person take 1 day.

Also: the 3% of tickets escalated to development (upgrade or software bug) take 26.2 days, against 5.7 for the rest.

Exhibit 5 · The handover network

One person sits in the middle of everything

This is the social graph of the help desk: who passes tickets to whom. Agent 2 handles 20% of all steps, is involved in 49% of the handovers between agents, and sits on 2.3× more of the shortest paths in the network than anyone else (betweenness centrality). That is a bottleneck in busy weeks and a risk when Agent 2 is away.

What we'd do

Five changes, in order of effort

Estimates come straight from the log and assume the team has the capacity to make the change; a pilot would test them.

1. Measure time-to-fix, not time-to-close

Report the fix date as the headline KPI and shorten the confirmation window to 7 days, with one-click reopening for customers.

Median time-to-close on paper: 39.9 → 13.7 days, with no change in the work.

2. A one-working-day pickup rule

Flag every ticket not taken in charge within one working day, and give someone ownership of the queue each day.

Average fix time: 11.1 → ~8.2 days if no ticket waited more than a day for pickup.

3. Give "Wait" a reason and a deadline

Require a reason code when parking a ticket, and sweep every parked ticket weekly. 27% of tickets pass through this status today.

Targets the group that takes 18.9 days to fix instead of 2.8.

4. Route tickets right the first time

Use the ticket's product, customer and description to suggest the right agent at intake. This is where an AI classifier pays off.

Fewer handovers: tickets solved by the first agent take 1 day, not 6–18.

5. Reduce the dependency on one person

Cross-train two agents on the ticket types that only Agent 2 handles today, and route those types to them first.

Less queueing in peak weeks, and no single point of failure.
Read this with care

Limits of this demo

  • The data is public and anonymised, so people, products and customers appear as codes, and it covers 2010–2014.
  • The comparisons in Exhibit 4 show association, not cause. Hard tickets may be parked or handed over because they're hard.
  • We only see the logged steps, not the ticket text. With a real client we'd add their categories, SLAs and costs per hour.
